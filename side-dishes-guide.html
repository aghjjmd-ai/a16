The Side Dishes Guide

A main course on its own is an ingredient. What you put beside it makes it dinner. Choosing sides is not guesswork once you know what each one is there to do.

What a side dish is for

Every side has one of three jobs. It fills, by adding starch. It freshens, by adding vegetables, acidity or crunch. Or it carries sauce, by giving the juices on the plate somewhere to go. A good dinner has at least the first two covered, and the best sides manage two jobs at once.

Choose by contrast

Look at the main and ask what it is missing.

  • Rich and creamy? Add something sharp: a dressed salad, pickled onions, steamed greens with lemon.
  • Lean and plain? Add something generous: buttery mash, roast potatoes, a creamy gratin.
  • Soft? Add crunch: raw slaw, toasted nuts, crisp bread.
  • Dry? Add moisture: a sauce, braised vegetables, beans in their liquor.
  • Brown all over? Add colour: anything green, red or orange.
The quick test. If you could describe the main and both sides with the same word, such as creamy, fried or beige, change one of them.

Keep the cooking sensible

The most common mistake is choosing two sides that both need the hob in the final five minutes. Spread the work across the kitchen.

  1. One in the oven. Roast potatoes, a gratin or a tray of vegetables look after themselves.
  2. One cold. A salad, slaw or dressed beans can be finished an hour ahead.
  3. At most one on the hob. Greens, peas or a quick sauce, done while the main rests.

Sides that can be made ahead

  • Day before: coleslaw without the dressing, gratin assembled and chilled, grain salads, pickled onions.
  • Morning: parboiled potatoes ready to roast, washed and dried salad leaves, vinaigrette in a jar.
  • Last minute only: green vegetables, dressed leaves, anything fried.

When in doubt

Potatoes in some form, one green vegetable and a wedge of lemon will sit happily beside nearly any main ever cooked. It is not exciting, and it is never wrong.
